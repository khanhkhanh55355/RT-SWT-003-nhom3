\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We evaluated how well GPT-4o can generate Gherkin BDD scenarios from user stories in a zero-shot setting. We tested it on 100 user story--scenario pairs sampled from the industrial dataset published by Rathnayake et al.\ (2026), covering four IntelligenceBank domains. We used pre-registered automated metrics and one-tailed statistical tests to assess performance.

For \textbf{RQ1}, we asked whether the generated scenarios are \emph{semantically close} to expert-written scenarios---specifically, whether the \textbf{median cosine similarity} exceeds \textbf{0.85}, measured with OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-large} embeddings. The results did \textbf{not} meet this threshold (median $=0.7742$, $p \approx 1.000$, Cliff's $\delta=-0.70$). In other words, even when the output sounds reasonable, it often differs in meaning from the expert reference under this metric.

For \textbf{RQ2}, we asked whether the generated scenarios are \emph{syntactically executable} Gherkin more than \textbf{80\%} of the time. Here, GPT-4o performed very strongly: \textbf{100\%} of generated scenarios were executable ($p=2.04 \times 10^{-10}$, Cohen's $h=0.93$). This shows the model can reliably produce structurally valid Given--When--Then scenarios.

Overall, GPT-4o \textbf{passed RQ2 but failed RQ1}: it consistently produces Gherkin that parses and runs, but the content is not consistently aligned with expert-written scenarios under the predefined semantic similarity threshold. Practically, this means teams can treat the model output as a solid \emph{starting draft}, but it still needs human review to confirm correctness, completeness, and alignment with domain expectations.

As far as we know, no prior work has put GPT-4o’s zero-shot Gherkin generation through this kind of end-to-end, repeatable check: automated metrics paired with statistical significance tests and judged against fixed, pre-registered thresholds on a replicated industrial sample. To make the study easy to verify and rerun, we also provide a public replication package (\url{https://github.com/khanhkhanh55355/RT-SWT-003-nhom3}) that captures the whole workflow—from sampling and API logging to the final hypothesis tests.

Looking ahead, three follow-up directions seem most promising. First, test \textbf{few-shot} and \textbf{chain-of-thought} prompting under the same dataset and acceptance thresholds to see whether semantic alignment improves without sacrificing syntactic executability. Second, move beyond a single happy-path scenario by adding \textbf{multi-step flows}, negative paths, and boundary cases. Third, explore \textbf{domain-specific fine-tuning} to bring semantic similarity closer to the 0.85 target while preserving the strong executable-syntax performance.
